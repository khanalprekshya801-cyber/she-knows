⚖️ Wait... Who Gets to Decide for Us?

Around the world, decisions made by governments and courts can change what women and girls are allowed to do, choose, and become.

🏠 Home

This isn't just about politics.

A rule written somewhere far away can sound like something that has nothing to do with everyday life.

But then that rule reaches a school, a hospital, a workplace, a courtroom — and suddenly it affects someone's actual life.

So here, we're looking at real cases and real decisions. Not just what the law says, but what happened to the people living with it.

🌍 Real Stories. Real Decisions.

🇦🇫 Afghanistan — Imagine Being Told You Can't Go to School

Imagine having a dream of becoming a doctor, teacher, engineer — or literally anything you want — and then being told that you can't continue your education because you're a girl.

That's what millions of Afghan girls have been facing since the Taliban returned to power.

🎓 What changed?
Girls have been barred from secondary education, and women have been excluded from universities. Women have also faced restrictions on employment, movement and participation in public life.

🚶 And it isn't only about school.
Restrictions have reached many ordinary parts of life. Women have faced rules affecting where they can go, which public spaces they can enter, how they dress, and whether they can work in certain areas.

🏛️ Who made these decisions?
The Taliban authorities governing Afghanistan have issued the decrees and policies behind these restrictions.

💬 What do the Taliban say?
Taliban officials say their policies follow their interpretation of Islamic law and Afghan culture.

💭 But here's the reality:

A girl doesn't get those lost school years back.

According to UN reporting in 2026, Afghanistan remains the only country in the world where girls are banned from secondary education and women are banned from university.

And when education, work and freedom of movement are restricted at the same time, it doesn't just affect someone's present. It can completely change her future.

🇺🇸 United States — The Doctor Who Helped a 10-Year-Old

This case became one of the biggest abortion stories in the United States after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade.

In 2022, a 10-year-old girl from Ohio became pregnant after being raped. She travelled to Indiana, where Dr. Caitlin Bernard provided an abortion.

And then the case became a massive national political fight.

👩‍⚕️ What happened to Dr. Bernard?
Indiana Attorney General Todd Rokita publicly criticized the doctor and announced an investigation into whether the abortion had been properly reported.

⚖️ So was the doctor simply sued for doing the abortion?
No — that's an important detail that often gets lost. The later lawsuit involved Indiana officials and the hospital system over the disclosure of patient information.

📢 Why did the case become so huge?
People across the U.S. started arguing about abortion access, rape, children's healthcare, privacy and what happens when a young girl cannot legally receive the care she needs in her own state.

💭 And this is the part that hits differently:

This wasn't an abstract debate.

There was an actual child at the center of it — a child who had already been through sexual violence and then had to travel to another state to receive medical care.

That's why this case became such a major part of the American conversation about abortion laws.

🇮🇳 India — A 17-Year-Old and a Decision About Her Own Body

This case happened very recently, and it is one of those stories where the legal words can almost make you forget that there is an actual person behind the case.

A 17-year-old survivor of sexual assault was nearly 29 weeks pregnant when the matter came before the Nagpur bench of the Bombay High Court.

⚖️ What did the court decide?
The court allowed the pregnancy to be terminated.

🗣️ What did the judge say?
The court made a strong statement about bodily autonomy, observing that a woman has the right over her own body and is the ultimate decision-maker about whether she wants an abortion.

💗 Why did this case matter?
The girl was a sexual-assault survivor, and the court considered her physical and mental well-being while deciding the case.

And honestly, that's what can get lost in cases like this.

When you see a court case written in a newspaper, it can feel like it's just another legal argument.

But behind the words "pregnancy," "petition" and "court order" was a teenager whose life had already been changed by violence.

The court's decision put her situation and bodily autonomy at the centre of the case.

Source:
Times of India — September 2026

🇵🇰 Pakistan — The Case of Maria Shahbaz

This case is about a 13-year-old Christian girl named Maria Shahbaz, and it has caused serious concern over child marriage, forced conversion and whether a minor's consent was actually protected.

Maria's family said she was abducted in 2025 and forced to convert to Islam and marry an older man. Her family then spent months fighting in court to get her back.

⚖️ What happened in court?
In July 2025, a lower court awarded custody of Maria to the man she was alleged to have been married to, after accepting documents that stated she was 18.

🏛️ What happened next?
Maria's family challenged the decision. In March 2026, Pakistan's Federal Constitutional Court upheld the ruling.

💬 Why did people object?
Rights groups and Maria's supporters argued that she was a minor and that the circumstances surrounding her alleged abduction, conversion and marriage needed much greater protection and scrutiny.

💭 Think about what that means.

When the person at the centre of a marriage case is only 13, questions about age, consent and protection become incredibly important.

This case also shows why documents and court decisions matter so much. A single ruling can decide where a child is sent and who has legal control over her life.

Sources:
U.S. Commission on International Religious Freedom
Federal Constitutional Court of Pakistan

💭 And this is why these stories matter.

Afghanistan, the United States, India and Pakistan are completely different countries. Their laws, governments and cultures are different too.

But look at what connects these stories.

A decision made by someone in power ended up affecting the education, healthcare, safety, freedom or future of a girl or woman.

That's why knowing what governments and courts are doing matters.

Who made the decision?

What actually happened?

And most importantly — what happened to the woman or girl at the centre of it?
