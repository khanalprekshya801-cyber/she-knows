🕰️ Women History Forgot

Some of these women changed science, medicine, music and history — but somehow, their names never became as famous as they deserved.

🏠 Home

You probably weren't taught all of this.

History has a funny way of remembering certain people and forgetting others.

Sometimes a woman's work was overlooked. Sometimes someone else received the recognition. Sometimes discrimination made it much harder for her to get credit.

So let's talk about some women whose stories deserve another look.

The stories we should have heard sooner

🧬 Henrietta Lacks

Image: Wikimedia Commons

Henrietta Lacks was a Black woman whose cells became one of the most important tools in modern medical research.

In 1951, while she was being treated for cervical cancer, doctors took cells from her tumor without telling her or getting her consent.

Those cells became known as HeLa cells. Unlike ordinary cells, they could keep reproducing in the laboratory. They were later used in research involving vaccines, cancer and genetics.

And here's what makes her story so complicated.

Henrietta died at just 31, while her cells continued to be used around the world.

Her family did not know for years how important her cells had become.

Her story eventually became a major part of conversations about medical consent, privacy and medical ethics.

🧪 Alice Ball

Image: Wikimedia Commons

Alice Ball was a chemist at the University of Hawaiʻi who made an important breakthrough in treating Hansen's disease, commonly known as leprosy.

She developed a way of making chaulmoogra oil usable as an injectable treatment. She was only 23 when she made the breakthrough.

Then she died suddenly in 1916, at only 24.

Here's the part that was unfair.

After her death, her research was used and published without her initially receiving the recognition she deserved.

Her contribution was eventually recognized, and the University of Hawaiʻi later honored her with Alice Augusta Ball Day.

⚛️ Lise Meitner

Image: Wikimedia Commons

Lise Meitner was an Austrian-Swedish physicist who played a crucial role in explaining nuclear fission.

She worked with chemist Otto Hahn for years. When nuclear fission was discovered, Meitner helped explain the physics behind what was happening.

But when the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded for the discovery of nuclear fission, it went to Otto Hahn.

And people still debate her recognition.

Meitner was not awarded the Nobel Prize for her role in the discovery, even though historians and scientists continue to discuss how important her contribution was.

🌌 Jocelyn Bell Burnell

Image: Wikimedia Commons

In 1967, Jocelyn Bell Burnell was a graduate student studying radio astronomy when she noticed a strange signal coming from space.

That signal turned out to be the first discovered pulsar — a rapidly rotating neutron star sending out beams of radiation.

Then came the Nobel Prize.

The 1974 Nobel Prize in Physics went to her supervisor Antony Hewish and astronomer Martin Ryle.

Bell Burnell was not one of the recipients.

She later received many major scientific honors.

🔬 Chien-Shiung Wu

Image: Wikimedia Commons — AIP Emilio Segrè Visual Archives

Chien-Shiung Wu was one of the most important experimental physicists of the twentieth century.

Her famous experiment provided powerful evidence that the law of parity conservation does not hold in weak nuclear interactions.

Basically, her experiment showed that nature wasn't behaving as symmetrically as scientists had expected.

Then came the Nobel Prize.

In 1957, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang for the theoretical work behind parity violation.

Wu's experimental contribution was not included among the Nobel recipients.

She later received many major honors and became one of the most respected experimental physicists of her time.

🚌 Claudette Colvin

Image: Wikimedia Commons

Claudette Colvin was only 15 years old when she refused to give up her seat on a segregated bus in Montgomery, Alabama, on March 2, 1955.

That happened nine months before Rosa Parks became famous for doing something similar.

Colvin was arrested and later became one of the plaintiffs in Browder v. Gayle, the case that helped end bus segregation in Montgomery.

So why don't more people know her name?

The movement's leaders chose not to make Colvin the public face of the bus boycott.

For decades, the simplified version of the story mostly remembered Rosa Parks.

🎸 Sister Rosetta Tharpe

Image: Wikimedia Commons

Before rock and roll became what we know today, Sister Rosetta Tharpe was already playing electric guitar with a sound that was completely ahead of its time.

She mixed gospel music with blues, jazz and energetic guitar playing, influencing generations of musicians.

And somehow, her name was left out for years.

Tharpe was hugely influential, but the popular story of rock and roll often focused on the men who came after her.

Her influence has become much more widely recognized, including her induction into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame.

🧬 Nettie Stevens

Nettie Stevens was an American geneticist whose research helped establish that chromosomes play a role in determining sex.

Her work involved studying chromosomes in insects and identifying important differences between male and female chromosome arrangements.

Her name isn't nearly as familiar as her discovery.

Stevens made major contributions to early genetics, but her work is much less commonly remembered outside scientific history.

She died in 1912 at just 50 years old, only a few years after some of her most important discoveries.

💭 So why were these women forgotten?

There isn't one simple answer.

Sometimes it was sexism. Sometimes racism. Sometimes institutions gave recognition to someone else. Sometimes history simply kept repeating the names that were already famous.

And that's exactly why these stories matter.

Being left out of a history book doesn't mean you weren't part of history.

These women were doing the work, making discoveries, challenging unfair systems and changing what came after them.

Maybe the next time you hear a famous historical story, you'll wonder who else was there — and whose name didn't make it into the headline.
